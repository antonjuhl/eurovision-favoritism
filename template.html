Data Visualization · Eurovision votes 1957–2023

Do neighbours give each other douze points?

Every country hands out a fixed set of points each year: 1–8, 10 and 12, and since 2016 two such sets, one from a jury and one from the public, so one country can now give another up to 24 points. This page measures how many extra points a country gives a specific other country compared with what that country normally receives, and asks which geographical clusters exist, how strong and consistent they are, and which country pairs drive them.

01Where are the clusters?

Each line joins two countries in the same cluster. The thicker the line, the more extra points they give each other on average. Hover a country to see who it favours most.

02How strong and how consistent is each cluster?

Each dot is one directed relationship inside a cluster, for example Denmark → Sweden. Further right means more extra points. Larger dots have more shared years behind them. The grey row shows every pair that crosses clusters, which is what “no favoritism” looks like in this data. The dark tick marks each row's average. Click a dot to see that pair year by year.

03Which pairs drive the pattern?

Colour by

Rows give points, columns receive them. The colour scale is fixed and centred on zero, so the same shade means the same thing in every cluster. Empty cells have fewer shared years than the minimum. Compare a cell with its mirror across the diagonal to see whether a relationship is mutual. Click a cell to see it year by year.

05Is it the public or the juries?

Since 2016 each country publishes its jury and televote results separately. Here the same favoritism measure is computed on each half alone, averaged over all within-cluster pairs. Uses the period filter, clipped to 2016 or later.

06How favoritism is measured

One number per country pair per show

For voter A and recipient B in a single show: share = points A gave B ÷ all points A gave. B's normal level is the average share B got from every other voter in that show. Favoritism is the difference, multiplied by 58 (the points in one 1–8, 10, 12 set), so it reads as extra points. +5 means A gave B about five points more than B usually gets from a country.

Why shares and not raw points

From 1975 to 2015 each country gave one set of 1–8, 10, 12 (58 points in total), so 12 was the maximum to any one country. Since 2016 the jury and the televote each give a full set, so a country hands out 116 points and can give a single country up to 24. Raw points would make every recent year look twice as friendly. Dividing by what each voter handed out, and expressing the result on one 58-point set, makes 2023 directly comparable with 1995. Early contests used other systems, and the same division handles those too.

Why the baseline leaves A out

If A's own vote counted towards B's normal level, a strong A → B vote would raise the bar it is measured against and hide part of the favoritism.

Reading the averages

Averages are over shows where both countries took part. Consistency is the share of those shows where the favoritism was above zero. Pairs below the minimum number of shared years are hidden everywhere, because a single year can produce an extreme value. Favoritism also captures shared taste, language, diaspora and song quality, so it shows patterns in voting, not why they happen.
